A hospital board that stops a decision made from a bad record

Built at HopHacks 2026. Here is the whole thing, explained without jargon.

The two problems

Problem one

Your medical history sits in pieces at every place that ever treated you — a hospital, an urgent care, a pharmacy. Nobody ever merges them. So the pieces disagree. One says you take 5mg. Another says 2.5mg. One lists a penicillin allergy. Another lists none.

Problem two

When a hospital is calm, a nurse has time to notice a record looks wrong and go check. When twenty-five patients arrive at once, nobody has that time. Decisions get made in seconds from whatever is on the screen.

Put those together and you get the thing that actually hurts people: a decision about where a patient goes that was completely logical — made from information that was wrong.

What happens, step by step

Follow one patient from the moment the button is pressed.

1

Patients arrive

Someone presses a button marked MASS CASUALTY. Twenty-five patients arrive at once.

2

Each department reports in

ICU      1 bed left, holding it for critical patients only
STEP-DOWN 3 beds open, accepting patients
ER       6 beds open, accepting patients

Each part of the hospital says what it can take right now.

3

The system picks a destination

For a 68-year-old who fainted at home, it chooses the step-down unit — not intensive care, because he does not appear to need it.

4

And it writes down why

step-down  BECAUSE  no blood thinner
                   vital signs stable
                   does not need intensive care

This is the part nothing else does. The decision has to name the facts it leaned on — so we know exactly what to go check.

5

Only those three facts get checked

Not the whole 700-page history. Just those three, looked up in every file this patient has anywhere.

This is why it is fast enough to run while patients are arriving.

6

Do the files agree?

A computer can spot that two numbers differ. What it cannot do with simple rules is tell that “Coumadin 5mg,” “warfarin 5 mg,” and “on anticoagulation” are the same fact written three ways — while “no anticoagulants” contradicts all three. That judgment is the one job we give the AI.

They disagree → held

The patient does not move. The row turns amber. Both versions appear on screen with the date each was written and where it came from.

It never says which one is right — because it genuinely does not know. It says a person has to resolve this first.

They agree → placed

The patient moves. The row turns green and a bed count drops by one.

Most patients go this way. Out of twenty-five, maybe three get stopped — which is the point. A short list worth reading, not a long list nobody reads.

What is pretend, and what is not

A student team cannot legally get real patient data in a weekend. So the hospital is invented — and we say so out loud. What matters is which parts are genuine.

Invented, openly

  • The bed counts, though they match what real American hospitals actually run at
  • The patients arriving, and what they came in with
  • The clock

Genuinely real

  • The record format — the same national standard hospitals use to send records to each other
  • The real medical codes inside those records
  • The disagreements: we planted them ourselves and kept a written list
  • Every source link on screen — click it and you get the actual record

That written list is the important bit. Because we know exactly what we hid, we can say how much got caught — 46 of 50 — instead of just claiming it works. Almost nothing at a student science fair can put a real number on itself.

We don’t flag every contradiction. We flag the ones that are about to change where a patient goes.

If you remember one sentence about this project, that’s the one.

Words you might hear us use

Held
A patient whose move has been paused because the records behind it disagree. The opposite is placed.
Provenance
Knowing exactly where a piece of information came from — which file, which hospital, what date. It’s why you can click a conflict and see the receipt.
Answer key
The list of problems we deliberately planted in the data, so results can be measured instead of guessed at.
Agent
People use this word as though it means something enormous. Here it’s a small piece of code with one narrow job — one looks at medications, one looks at dates, one looks at whether the departments have room.
